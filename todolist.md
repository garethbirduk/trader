- **Two-tier gossip: headlines vs details.** Today every lead carries the full payload (qty, price, counterparty, subject, source chain, hop count, confidence). The richer design splits a lead into:
  - **Headline** — cheap to share, vague payload. "I hear Boyce has radios." "Del Boy bribed a copper at the market." Free to exchange in chats; the default thing that propagates through the gossip layer. The headline is just the *subject* (actor + item kind, or actor + event type).
  - **Detail** — everything that makes the information *actionable*: the numerics (qty, price, fine amount), the **source chain** ("Rodney told me Mickey said Denzil sold Boyce some radios"), the **hop count**, and crucially the **confidence (warm/cold)**. Without the detail tier, the asker doesn't know if their headline is firsthand or has been through four mouths — they can't gauge reliability before acting.
  - Unlocked by paying a cost AND spending time with the informant. Pubs use the natural unit: buy them a drink (~£3) and sit for an hour. After the chat, the detail tier becomes visible to the asker.
  - The credibility-is-paid-for framing matters: a free headline of "Boyce has radios" could be six hops cold from a misheard rumour, but you wouldn't know until you'd invested the £3 + hour to walk back the chain. That's the *real* asset behind information — not the numbers, but the chain that tells you whether to trust them.
  - The info-trader cast (Mike, Sid, Denzil, Albert) are the natural detail-holders — they accumulate the full pictures; ordinary actors only see the headlines until they pay.
  - Composes with Stage 4's clarification action: clarification today freely reveals the target's matching lead. Under two-tier, clarification reveals the **headline** for free; the detail tier still needs the drink-and-chat investment.
  - This gives the player something to actually DO with their cash and their hours. "I have £3 — I'll go buy Trigger a pint and find out who really said what about Boyce's radios." Information becomes an asset class.
  - Implementation hint: rather than two row types, add `details_revealed_to: ReadonlySet<actorId>` on each lead (or a separate `lead_disclosures(lead_id, actor_id, revealed_at_day)` table). - **Multiple independent knowledge axes per actor — not just one, not just two.** Today's `bidderProfile` partly recognises this: `appraisalAccuracy` is one axis per category, `flawTypeDetection` is a *separate* axis per flaw type. The fact that these are already split makes the right pattern obvious — every facet of "knowing what you're looking at" is a separate cognitive skill. The realistic decomposition:
  - **1. Identification** — *what is this thing?* Rolex vs Rulex; real Persian rug vs a printed-pattern carpet; first-edition Beatles LP vs a re-press. Per item-kind (or per pair of confusable item-kinds). An idiot has no idea; a specialist *knows*. Resolution: **inspection**, filtered by the inspector's id-skill — low skill means inspection returns a *guess* with no flag that it might be wrong.
  - **2. Tier / condition assessment** — *given I know what it is, how good is its state?* This Rolex is mint, scratched, or knackered. This table is good or fair or shoddy. Per category. Plausibly tracks separately from identification — you might know it's a Rolex but not know what years had crown-guards or which pre-loved scuffs are deal-breakers. Today's `appraisalAccuracy` does some of this work but doesn't separate it from price-knowledge.
  - **3. Flaw detection** — *is anything wrong with it?* Stolen, fake, faulty, dangerous, wrong-season, wrong-market, scam-bait. The engine already has this as a separate axis (`flawTypeDetection`, per flaw type). A fence is sharp on "stolen" but mediocre on "fake"; an electrician is sharp on "faulty"; a customs guy is sharp on "wrong-market." Different sub-skills per flaw type, already in the schema.
  - **4. Market price knowledge** — *given identity + tier + no flaws, what's the going rate?* Mickey-the-street-trader knows a good Rulex sells for £23–£25 and a good Rolex sells for £2000–£2025 — both tight bands. He just can't tell which one he's holding. So he's a useful **price oracle** but a useless **authenticator** or **condition assessor** or **flaw spotter**. An auction valuer might inverse — sharp on id and condition, broad-band on retail because they don't move the goods.
  - **5. Customer fit** — *who would buy this?* Already in the engine via `customerTypes` on items + bidder profiles. A specialist knows the buyer pool; a generalist doesn't. Determines whether a found lot is a fit for known onward demand or a punt.
  - **Each skill is unlocked by a different consultation.** "Is it real?" → ask an identifier. "How's the condition?" → ask a condition-assessor. "Is it hot?" → ask a fence. "What's it sell for?" → ask a price oracle. "Who buys this?" → ask a market-savvy contact. Costs aggregate: a thorough due-diligence pass might cost £15 + 5 hours across five visits, which is fine — that itself becomes a decision ("is this lot worth the workup?"). Each visit £3 + 1h with someone at the venue.
  - **Per-NPC profile becomes a vector, not a number.** Each actor gets a small grid: rows = categories or item-kinds, columns = each skill axis. Mickey: low id, low condition, low flaw, **high price** for watches; everything else baseline. Boyce: high id, high condition, moderate price, baseline flaw for furniture. The auction clerk: high all-round, but charges accordingly. Pure information-collector NPCs like Albert sit at zero across the trade axes but at high gossip-yield — they don't appraise, they remember.
  - **The mis-skill cascades.** If you consult Mickey for ID on a Rolex (low skill) and he says "it's a Rulex," you might pay him for the cheap-tier price band (£23–£25) and walk away thinking you've found a £25 retail item — when you're actually holding £2000 of watch. The expected value of consulting a bad expert isn't just "wider band" — it can be *catastrophically wrong*. That's a real game-design wrinkle: cheap experts are sometimes worse than no expert because they give false confidence.
  - **Player learns from observed outcomes.** If Mickey's id called a watch a Rulex and you later resold it as a confirmed Rolex (because Boyce identified it properly and the auction confirmed), Mickey's id-skill score in your *observation log* drops. You stop asking him. The skill grid is the engine's truth; the player's observation log is their working estimate. Reading the gap between the two is the real meta-game — figuring out who actually knows what.
  - **Implementation shape.** Expand `bidderProfile` from `{appraisalAccuracy, flawTypeDetection}` to a vector: `{idAccuracy, conditionAccuracy, flawDetection, priceAccuracy, customerFitAccuracy}`, each keyed by category (or, for `flawDetection`, by flaw type as today). Per-(actor, lot) consultation history persists which actor evaluated which lot on which axis, with what answer. The viewer's `Knows` panel shows "for this lot in your bag: 3 consultations done, 2 axes still uncertain" — the notebook surfaces what's left to learn.

  - **Id-skill decomposes into four orthogonal sub-skills (v2 direction).** What v1 stores as a single `idAccuracy` scalar is really four separate competences. An actor can be sharp on some and clueless on others, and the cast becomes more believable when those gaps are individually expressible:
    1. **Resolution — how many sub-ranges does the actor perceive within this category?** A novice sees "watches: one big blur." A pawnbroker partitions into 4 brand bands. A specialist sees 4 brands × 12 sub-models each. Today implicit; v2 makes it per-actor explicit as a "perceived kinds within category" subset of the global kind list. Limits everything downstream — you can't place a lot in a band you don't perceive.
    2. **Placement — given the partitioning the actor can see, which sub-range does this lot fall in?** This is what `idAccuracy` does today, refined from pass/fail-per-pair to N-way classification.
    3. **Width — how tightly does the actor know each sub-range's price bounds?** Generalist: "Tissot, £500–£5k somewhere." Specialist: "Tissot Le Locle, £550–£650." Today this lives in `priceAccuracy` per-category; v2 keys it per-brand-range.
    4. **Condition impact — how does mint vs scratched vs broken actually move the price for this specific band?** A scratched Rolex retains far more value than a scratched Casio; the discount curve is brand-specific. Partially captured today by `conditionAccuracy` (knowing the tier), but the "what the tier DOES to the price for this band" part is a new axis a repair-shop electrician can be clueless on while still placing the lot correctly.

    The cast gains believability through the gaps these four expose: a pawnbroker who classifies perfectly but can't sub-divide Rolex (high Q2, low Q1 on Rolex internals); a generalist who knows the partition but is fuzzy on band widths (Q1/Q2 high, Q3 low); a specialist who places kit correctly but doesn't grasp condition impact (Q1/Q2/Q3 sharp, Q4 low). None of those archetypes are expressible with a single id scalar.

  - **Drop brand+model — id IS the actor's band partition (v2 direction).** The v1 schema `confusable_item_pairs(kind_a, kind_b, difficulty)` and the brand-range refinement above both still encode named identities (Rolex, Tissot, Rulex). Brand names do no engine work — they're labels on price bands. The cleaner model: items don't need brand identifiers at all; the engine stores only a true RRP and a category, and each actor carries their **own mental partition** of that category's price universe.
    - **Per (actor, category): a list of bands** `(low, high)` representing how the actor partitions the category's global RRP range. The structure encodes both resolution and coverage in one place:
      - Idiot watches: `[(5, 50000)]` — one band, total blur.
      - Novice watches: `[(5, 5000), (5000, 50000)]` — cheap vs best.
      - Skilled watches: `[(5, 50), (100, 250), (500, 2000), (2000, 10000), (10000, 50000)]` — five bands with gaps in between.
      - An idiot can also have a *small* coverage: `[(2000, 10000)]` is one band that only knows mid-range; encountering a £50 watch or a £35k watch falls outside what they perceive.
    - **Within-band tightness** is a second-tier skill — given the actor has placed the lot in band `(500, 2000)`, how tightly can they quote it? Specialist narrows to `(1000, 1200)`; generalist quotes the whole band. Per-(actor, category) scalar or per-(actor, band) for finer control.
    - **The v1 `id` and `price` axes collapse into one** continuous "where on the category's price axis does this sit" competence with two parameters: band-list + within-band tightness. No more separate identification.
    - **Schema implication**: drop `confusable_item_pairs` entirely. Drop brand-named item kinds (`rolex`, `rulex`). Replace with one `actor_category_bands(actor_id, category, low, high)` table and a `band_tightness` skill axis. Items just carry true RRP + category.
    - **Condition splits cleanly into two sub-skills, one transferable, one not.**
      - **Detection** — *is it broken / scratched / mint?* Visual pattern recognition; mostly **category-agnostic and transferable**. An old hand who spots damage on watches can mostly spot it on hi-fis and shirts too. Modelled as a per-actor default with optional per-category overrides — the default is the transferable component, overrides capture category-specific learning (electronics damage cues are different from textile damage cues).
      - **Impact** — *given it's broken, what multiplier applies to price?* Per-category, per-tier. Knowing a broken Rolex is ×0.5 (collector still wants it) vs a broken Casio is ×0.1 (useless) is category-specific knowledge; doesn't transfer.
    - **The four core price-axis skills (v2 core)**:
      1. **Band identification** — how many bands the actor perceives in this category, AND placing the lot into the right one. Partition and placement read as one act from the actor's POV.
      2. **Band tightness** — narrowing within the placed band.
      3. **Condition impact** — knowledge of the tier multipliers (broken = ×0.1 or ×0.5?).
      4. **Condition detection** — reading the tier off the lot in front of you.

      (1)+(2) produce the price band; (3)+(4) modulate it. That's the whole "where on the price axis does this lot sit?" decomposition. **Flaw-detection and customer-fit live outside this core** — they're orthogonal side-channel modifiers on the final price (stolen knocks the buyer's ceiling; bad customer fit shreds onward margin), but they don't belong in the price-axis competence itself.
    - **Transferable defaults** become the engine's natural way of expressing cross-category skill. Every axis has a per-actor scalar default that applies unless a per-category override exists. An actor's "general competence" is captured by their defaults; their specialism by their overrides. A retired pawnbroker's high default condition-detection transfers to everything they pick up; their watches-specific band partition doesn't.

  - **Canonical worked example: the watch.** This is the entire system in one OFAH beat.
    1. **Del Boy pulls a clearance** at Mrs Smith's house (paid £500, gets 8 items including a watch — call the cost basis on the watch £25). Del's initial belief about the watch: *"it's a watch"* — band £20–£12000, spanning every plausible (id, condition, going-rate) combination.
    2. **Del consults Rodney on CONDITION.** Cost: £3 + 1h. Rodney says "it's mint!" *(Rodney's condition skill on watches is moderate; he's wrong — it's actually scratched, but Del doesn't know that.)* Del's belief narrows: mint Rulex £100–£200 OR mint Rolex £8000–£12000 → combined £100–£12000.
    3. **Del consults Albert on IDENTITY.** Cost: £3 + 1h. Albert says "Rulex." *(Albert can't tell a Rolex from a Rulex; he's wrong, but Del doesn't know that.)* Del's belief narrows: mint Rulex £100–£200.
    4. **Del consults Mickey on PRICE.** Cost: £3 + 1h. Mickey knows the streets and recites tight bands: mint Rulex £100–£110, scratched Rulex £20–£25, mint Rolex £10000–£10200, scratched Rolex £3800–£3900. *Del specifically asks about mint Rulex.* Returns: £100–£110. *(Del deliberately did NOT ask Grandad — Grandad's price knowledge on watches is rubbish, would have quoted £20000–£30000. Del's choice-of-expert is itself a knowledge act.)*
    5. **Del's final belief: mint Rulex worth £100–£110.** Cost so far: £25 + £9 = £34. Three hours spent.
    6. **Del sells to Boyce.** Del's anchor: *"This watch retails at £110. To you, £75."* Boyce instantly recognised it as a Rolex on sight (his ID skill on watches is high). Boyce doesn't know the condition. Boyce's belief: mint Rolex £8000–£12000 OR scratched Rolex £2000–£4000 → band £2000–£12000. Boyce's ceiling is *enormously* above Del's ask. **But Boyce doesn't reveal that.** Haggle plays inside Del's frame: "£50" — "£70" — "£60" — done. Del walks away happy (£60 received, £25+£9 invested, £26 profit). Boyce walks away with a Rolex for £60.
    7. **Boyce now refines his beliefs on his new watch.** Consults a checker on condition. Finds out it's scratched (Rodney was wrong). Boyce's belief: scratched Rolex £2000–£4000. Consults Mickey on price for scratched Rolex: £3800–£3900. Boyce's final belief: scratched Rolex worth £3800–£3900. Cost basis on Boyce's side: £60 + £6 in consults.
    8. **Boyce sells to the jewellers.** The jewellers are informed (their job). Haggle: Boyce asks "£3000" — jeweller offers "£1500" — Boyce counters "£2500" — jeweller "£2000" — done. Boyce profits £2000 − £66 = **£1934** on a watch he bought for £60 four hours earlier.
    9. **The whole arc in one ratio.** Del's profit £26 from his clearance haul slice; Boyce's profit £1934 from one well-IDed item. The £1908 gap is the information surplus — paid by Del (without him knowing) into Boyce's pocket. *And Del still feels he had a good day.* That's the entire game.
  - **What this example teaches the design.** (a) Belief is *built* axis by axis through paid consultations. (b) Each consultation can be *wrong*; the player doesn't know it until contradiction or resale. (c) The player chooses *which* expert to consult (and which to avoid) — choice-of-expert is meta-skill. (d) The seller's belief frame anchors the haggle; an informed buyer plays inside it and banks the surplus invisibly. (e) The buyer's *next* trade refines their own belief and unlocks the surplus value via better-informed sale. (f) The entire arc from clearance → consultation chain → asymmetric sale → refined consultation → onward sale is what *one watch* looks like as a gameplay loop. Multiple loops running in parallel = the world.

    - **Boyce's accounting.** £1500 cost + £6 consult = £1506 in. £2000 out. **£494 profit.**
    - **Net surplus distribution.** Information surplus from the chain (true value ~£2000–£3900 vs Del's £25 acquisition cost): roughly £2000 net surplus. Del captures £1466 (73%); Boyce captures £494 (25%); jewellers a small slice on the onward retail margin (2%). Compared to the naive-anchor example where Boyce captured £1934 of a similar gap: **the anchor flips who keeps the money.**
  - **What the variant teaches.** (g) The ask is a *strategic* layer on top of the belief — sellers can anchor inside or outside their honest band, hedging against their own uncertainty about their own beliefs. A seller who suspects they might be wrong about identity in their *favour* (it might be more valuable than they think) anchors HIGH. A seller worried they're wrong against them anchors LOW. (h) The buyer's response is also strategic: they can engage at a higher floor if their belief band gives positive expected value across the uncertainty. (i) The haggle isn't just two parties' beliefs colliding — it's two parties' *anchored asks* colliding, with anchors that may or may not match honest belief. (j) **The same information state can produce very different outcomes depending on player strategy.** This is the real depth: belief is the input; the ask is the *decision*.
  - **Design implication: ask anchoring is a player-controllable knob.** Today the autonomy picks the seller's open at `acquiredUnitPrice × sellerTargetMarkup` (cost-anchored). Under this richer model it should be `seller_belief_band.high × confidence_multiplier` — and the multiplier reflects how aggressive the seller is. A "test the water" seller adds 50–100% to their honest top-of-band. A naive / quick-sale seller anchors at their honest mid. NPC personalities could vary on this axis (Boyce: high anchor strategist; Trigger: anchors at honest middle; Del: opportunistic — anchors high when uncertain about identity). The player chooses theirs each negotiation.
  - **All these axes ultimately collapse into ONE working belief per item: "what's the max I can extract?"** This is the seller's mental band, built from a product of three sub-beliefs:
    1. **Identity belief** — "I think it's a Rolex." (Could be wrong: actually a Rulex.)
    2. **Condition belief** — "I think it's in good condition." (Could be wrong: actually shoddy.)
    3. **Going-rate belief** — "I think [Rolex + good] sells for £500–£4000." (Could be wrong: actually £8000–£12000.)
  - **The combined band is wider than any single sub-belief's range.** Mathematically a mixture of distributions: for each plausible (identity, condition) pair the seller thinks is possible, the going-rate band for that pair contributes. Worst-case seller: thinks it could be a Rolex OR a Rulex (50/50), so his band stretches from "Rulex price range" to "Rolex price range" — a chasm. Confident seller: 95% sure it's a Rolex, 95% sure of condition, 95% sure of going-rate — tight band.
  - **Each sub-belief can be catastrophically wrong, and the haggle anchors on the seller's belief, not the truth.**
    - *Seller underestimates*: Del Boy paid £25 thinking it's a Rulex. He believes Rulex's go for £100 retail. He asks Boyce £75 ("to you, I can sell for 75"). Boyce — who immediately recognised the Rolex worth £9000 — *plays along inside Del's belief frame*, haggles down to £60, walks away with £8940 of invisible margin. Del walks away happy: £25 paid, £60 received, "£35 profit, nice." Del never learns. Boyce never tells. The asymmetric knowledge converts directly into Boyce's profit.
    - *Seller overestimates*: thinks it's a £2000 Rolex; it's actually a broken Rulex. Asks £2000. Every buyer (correctly) walks. Item sits in his bag forever.
    - *Hidden flaws* (stolen / faulty / wrong-market) compound the error multiplicatively.
  - **The price clears at the LESSER-informed party's belief frame.** When seller and buyer have different beliefs about the same item, the haggle anchors on whoever's wrong in the seller's favour or wrong in the buyer's favour:
    - *Both informed → near true value.* (Standard haggle, narrow gap, clears mid-band.)
    - *Seller informed, buyer ignorant → buyer can't afford / walks.* (Seller's ask is high; buyer's ceiling is low because they think it's worth less.)
    - *Seller ignorant, buyer informed → trade clears below true value; buyer banks the surplus.* (The Del/Boyce case. Buyer keeps offers inside seller's frame, never reveals their true ceiling.)
    - *Both ignorant → trade clears around joint hallucinated value, both potentially happy or both later catastrophically wrong.* (Comedy of errors.)
  - **The trade opportunity IS the mis-calibration.** Asymmetric knowledge between buyer and seller is exactly how value flows from the unknowing to the knowing. The whole "Del Boy sells Boyce a £9000 watch for £60 because Del thinks it's a Rulex" arc is exactly this dynamic — the engine should make those beats producible and the player should be able to BE either party.
  - **Mechanically: each party's haggle anchors use THEIR OWN belief band, not the truth.** Today the seller's floor is `lot.acquiredUnitPrice` (cost-anchored). It should become their max-extraction *band* (belief-anchored). Today the buyer's ceiling is their appraisal × fraction (truth-anchored via noisy `valuation`). It should become their max-acquisition *band* (also belief-anchored via their own id/condition/flaw beliefs). Both parties haggle inside what *they think is fair*. The structural payoff is that an informed-but-quiet buyer can converge on a price the seller is happy with while still capturing the knowledge surplus, exactly as Boyce does to Del.
  - **Cost basis is separate from the belief.** What you paid is sunk; it doesn't shape your "max extraction" estimate. It shapes a separate consideration ("am I about to take a loss?"). The player can decide to cut losses below cost when they realise they were duped. So the view shows cost as a sidebar reference (loss/break-even/profit marker), not as one of "the prices."
  - **Channels modify but don't replace the belief.** "Max extraction" varies by channel — selling to Mickey (low id) vs Boyce (high id) vs the auction-house gallery yields different prices for the same item because each counterparty (or each channel's pricing model) processes the same goods differently. The player's optimisation: pick the channel where YOUR knowledge is highest AND the counterparty's calibration is favourable. The single working belief feeds per-channel estimates; the view can show the best channel and the spread across channels.
  - **The viewer's job collapses to two things per lot:**
    1. **Your current best estimate of max extraction** — a confidence band, with the sub-beliefs (id, condition, going-rate, flaws) listed beneath as the components. Each sub-belief is paid to narrow.
    2. **Cost basis as a reference** — single number, marks where break-even is on the extraction band.
  - **Conflict surfaces as diverging sub-beliefs, not diverging output bands.** When Mickey says "Rulex" and Boyce says "Rolex," the player sees TWO id-sub-beliefs in conflict ("Mickey: Rulex, conf 0.3; Boyce: Rolex, conf 0.9") and the combined band stretches across both possible identities. The conflict is structurally visible — the player can drill into which expert was wrong by tracking which sub-belief contributed which slice of the band.
  - **Implementation shape.** Persist sub-beliefs per (actor, lot): `actor_beliefs(actor_id, lot_id, axis, value, confidence, sourced_from_actor_id, day_acquired)`. Each consultation writes a row. The combined extraction band is computed on-the-fly by walking the rows for that lot and integrating across plausible (id, condition, flaw) combinations weighted by the actor's confidence in each. The single number "your band" is just the marginal of that mixture. Cost basis stays on the stock_lot row as today. Channel-specific extraction estimates compose per-counterparty bidder profiles on top of the lot's belief.
