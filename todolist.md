- **Witnessed events become gossip-able leads.** A bribe (or any "notable" event) seen by a third party at the venue spawns a `rep`-style lead in each witness's bag, with the actors + amount + context (what was being sold, what the bribe was for). - **Two-tier gossip: headlines vs details.** Today every lead carries the full payload (qty, price, counterparty, subject, source chain, hop count, confidence). The richer design splits a lead into:
  - **Headline** — cheap to share, vague payload. "I hear Boyce has radios." "Del Boy bribed a copper at the market." Free to exchange in chats; the default thing that propagates through the gossip layer. The headline is just the *subject* (actor + item kind, or actor + event type).
  - **Detail** — everything that makes the information *actionable*: the numerics (qty, price, fine amount), the **source chain** ("Rodney told me Mickey said Denzil sold Boyce some radios"), the **hop count**, and crucially the **confidence (warm/cold)**. Without the detail tier, the asker doesn't know if their headline is firsthand or has been through four mouths — they can't gauge reliability before acting.
  - Unlocked by paying a cost AND spending time with the informant. Pubs use the natural unit: buy them a drink (~£3) and sit for an hour. After the chat, the detail tier becomes visible to the asker.
  - The credibility-is-paid-for framing matters: a free headline of "Boyce has radios" could be six hops cold from a misheard rumour, but you wouldn't know until you'd invested the £3 + hour to walk back the chain. That's the *real* asset behind information — not the numbers, but the chain that tells you whether to trust them.
  - The info-trader cast (Mike, Sid, Denzil, Albert) are the natural detail-holders — they accumulate the full pictures; ordinary actors only see the headlines until they pay.
  - Composes with Stage 4's clarification action: clarification today freely reveals the target's matching lead. Under two-tier, clarification reveals the **headline** for free; the detail tier still needs the drink-and-chat investment.
  - This gives the player something to actually DO with their cash and their hours. "I have £3 — I'll go buy Trigger a pint and find out who really said what about Boyce's radios." Information becomes an asset class.
  - Implementation hint: rather than two row types, add `details_revealed_to: ReadonlySet<actorId>` on each lead (or a separate `lead_disclosures(lead_id, actor_id, revealed_at_day)` table). When actor X pays for clarification on Y's lead L, add X to that set. Filter the viewer + autonomy reads by that set: if you're not on the disclosure list, you see headline only.
  - Knobs: per-info-trader drink cost, chat duration, what fields are gated (today's data: numerics + source-chain + confidence — basically everything but the subject identity).

- **A "notes" / organiser layer for what the player knows.** The existing `ActorKnows` Timeline / By-item / By-person groupings are mechanical, not actionable. The player needs a notebook that organises leads by **what's useful to THEM specifically** — keyed off their own bag and their own wishlist. Two natural buckets:
  - **Stock I have → who wants it.** Cross-reference my inventory against demand-side leads in my bag. "I have Radios — Trigger told me Boyce wants Radios." That row is immediately actionable: head to Boyce, propose a deal. Sort by potential margin (their gossiped paying-price minus my acquired cost).
  - **Stock I want → who has it.** Cross-reference my buyers' needs (or my own gaps) against supply-side leads. "I want Tables — Monkey Harris told me Mickey has Tables to sell." If the headline looks promising, it might be worth £3 + an hour to buy MH a drink and unlock the detail: "Mickey has 200 GOOD tables." The notes view should make that upgrade cost-effective decision visible at a glance.
  - **Counterparty blind spots make the call.** The bidder-profile-accuracy data we already track tells you who undervalues what. If Mickey has poor furniture appraisal (low accuracy on the `furniture` category), the note should flag that: "Mickey has tables AND he doesn't know good furniture — sell him stuff cheap, or buy his tables cheap." The notebook surfaces both the lead AND the exploitable knowledge gap.
  - All derivable from existing data: actor inventory, lead bag (qty, price, side, subject, source chain), bidder profiles (per-category accuracy), and demand-side leads. No new schema, just a new view that joins them and ranks rows by expected profit or value-of-information.
  - Not sure of the exact shape yet — these are the kinds of rows the notebook needs to show. Design properly once two-tier gossip is in (because headline-vs-detail makes the £3 upgrade decision real).

- **House clearance opportunities — a "mystery box" stock channel that's also a race.** Currently dealers get stock via pool claims, starter stock, auction wins, and pubdeals. Add a fourth: pay a fixed fee (e.g. £500) for a randomised clearance haul. *"Mrs Smith popped her clogs — £500 to clear her house, you take what's there."*
  - **Discovery is gated like the auction docket.** New clearance opportunities appear in the *newspaper* each morning. Knowing what's available requires being at Sid's / a newsagent during paper hours, or gossiping with someone who's read it. Same `auction-listing-knowledge.ts` shape — clearances are listings, not a free option.
  - **First-come-first-served on a scheduled hour, not arrival.** The mechanic is *book it*: make a phone call from a pub (or anywhere with a phone — could later split this into "pubs only" or extend to homes/cafes) to arrange a clearance time. Cost: 1 hour (the call + the visit later) plus the £500 fee at the time of clearance. Booking creates a row `{clearanceId, bookerActorId, scheduledDay, scheduledHour}`. Whichever booker's scheduled hour comes *first* wins the goods. Anyone scheduled later turns up to nothing — fee not paid (they never got the goods), but they wasted the hour.
  - **The phone call is overheard.** Anyone at the venue when Del Boy phones Mrs Smith hears something. Witnesses (`getActorsAtLocation` excluding the booker) get a fresh gossip lead about the call. Two-tier:
    - **Headline** (free, propagates): *"Del Boy is going to buy a house clearance."* You know he's after one, you don't know which or when.
    - **Detail** (drink + hour with a witness, ~£3 + 1h): *"Del Boy is clearing Mrs Smith's house at 4pm."* Now you have the address, the time, the booker. Now you can race.
  - **Racing the booker.** Whoever has the detail can phone in their own earlier booking and beat the original to it. Cinematic: Boyce overhears Del Boy at the Nag's at 11am → buys Trigger a pint and unlocks the detail by midday → phones for Mrs Smith's clearance at 1pm → arrives 2pm and walks out with the goods → Del Boy turns up at 4pm to an empty house and a wasted hour. The whole gameplay loop in one example.
  - **Mix and tier of the haul.** Bundle is 3–7 lots drawn from the everyday catalogue with tier-weighted randomness skewed residential — e.g. *2× good tables, 1× poor sofa, 1× new hi-fi, 1× poor wardrobe*. Furniture/electrical/decor heavy; no commercial categories. Tier mix favours middle (10% mint, 20% good, 35% fair, 25% shoddy, 10% broken). Items land in the actor's lockup.
  - **Easter eggs occasionally show up** in clearances — 1–2% chance of a flavoured rare item ("brass elephant from India", "first-edition Beatles LP") in addition to the standard mixed bag. Worth more on retail than the £500 fee on a good day.
  - **NPC autonomy:** dealers with cash and empty bags should be candidates for booking clearances (compete with player for the listings). Booking decision is driven by the planner — cash threshold, current bag size, holding leads about the clearance details.
  - **Economics + composition.** Fee → off-map ledger (cash conservation stays clean). Composes with Stage 7's regional-clearance scheduler (which feeds auction lots from "estate clearance in Bromley" etc); think of THESE as the local equivalent — the ones close enough to clear yourself instead of letting Sotheby's take them. The headline-detail mechanic for the phone call is the *prototype* application of the two-tier gossip idea above; building this is also building the two-tier infrastructure.

Could surface as a per-actor `trade_orientation: "wholesale" | "retail"` tag, planner-aware.

- **Multiple independent knowledge axes per actor — not just one, not just two.** Today's `bidderProfile` partly recognises this: `appraisalAccuracy` is one axis per category, `flawTypeDetection` is a *separate* axis per flaw type. The fact that these are already split makes the right pattern obvious — every facet of "knowing what you're looking at" is a separate cognitive skill. The realistic decomposition:
  - **1. Identification** — *what is this thing?* Rolex vs Rulex; real Persian rug vs a printed-pattern carpet; first-edition Beatles LP vs a re-press. Per item-kind (or per pair of confusable item-kinds). An idiot has no idea; a specialist *knows*. Resolution: **inspection**, filtered by the inspector's id-skill — low skill means inspection returns a *guess* with no flag that it might be wrong.
  - **2. Tier / condition assessment** — *given I know what it is, how good is its state?* This Rolex is mint, scratched, or knackered. This table is good or fair or shoddy. Per category. Plausibly tracks separately from identification — you might know it's a Rolex but not know what years had crown-guards or which pre-loved scuffs are deal-breakers. Today's `appraisalAccuracy` does some of this work but doesn't separate it from price-knowledge.
  - **3. Flaw detection** — *is anything wrong with it?* Stolen, fake, faulty, dangerous, wrong-season, wrong-market, scam-bait. The engine already has this as a separate axis (`flawTypeDetection`, per flaw type). A fence is sharp on "stolen" but mediocre on "fake"; an electrician is sharp on "faulty"; a customs guy is sharp on "wrong-market." Different sub-skills per flaw type, already in the schema.
  - **4. Market price knowledge** — *given identity + tier + no flaws, what's the going rate?* Mickey-the-street-trader knows a good Rulex sells for £23–£25 and a good Rolex sells for £2000–£2025 — both tight bands. He just can't tell which one he's holding. So he's a useful **price oracle** but a useless **authenticator** or **condition assessor** or **flaw spotter**. An auction valuer might inverse — sharp on id and condition, broad-band on retail because they don't move the goods.
  - **5. Customer fit** — *who would buy this?* Already in the engine via `customerTypes` on items + bidder profiles. A specialist knows the buyer pool; a generalist doesn't. Determines whether a found lot is a fit for known onward demand or a punt.
  - **Each skill is unlocked by a different consultation.** "Is it real?" → ask an identifier. "How's the condition?" → ask a condition-assessor. "Is it hot?" → ask a fence. "What's it sell for?" → ask a price oracle. "Who buys this?" → ask a market-savvy contact. Costs aggregate: a thorough due-diligence pass might cost £15 + 5 hours across five visits, which is fine — that itself becomes a decision ("is this lot worth the workup?"). Each visit £3 + 1h with someone at the venue.
  - **Per-NPC profile becomes a vector, not a number.** Each actor gets a small grid: rows = categories or item-kinds, columns = each skill axis. Mickey: low id, low condition, low flaw, **high price** for watches; everything else baseline. Boyce: high id, high condition, moderate price, baseline flaw for furniture. The auction clerk: high all-round, but charges accordingly. Pure information-collector NPCs like Albert sit at zero across the trade axes but at high gossip-yield — they don't appraise, they remember.
  - **The mis-skill cascades.** If you consult Mickey for ID on a Rolex (low skill) and he says "it's a Rulex," you might pay him for the cheap-tier price band (£23–£25) and walk away thinking you've found a £25 retail item — when you're actually holding £2000 of watch. The expected value of consulting a bad expert isn't just "wider band" — it can be *catastrophically wrong*. That's a real game-design wrinkle: cheap experts are sometimes worse than no expert because they give false confidence.
  - **Player learns from observed outcomes.** If Mickey's id called a watch a Rulex and you later resold it as a confirmed Rolex (because Boyce identified it properly and the auction confirmed), Mickey's id-skill score in your *observation log* drops. You stop asking him. The skill grid is the engine's truth; the player's observation log is their working estimate. Reading the gap between the two is the real meta-game — figuring out who actually knows what.
  - **Implementation shape.** Expand `bidderProfile` from `{appraisalAccuracy, flawTypeDetection}` to a vector: `{idAccuracy, conditionAccuracy, flawDetection, priceAccuracy, customerFitAccuracy}`, each keyed by category (or, for `flawDetection`, by flaw type as today). Per-(actor, lot) consultation history persists which actor evaluated which lot on which axis, with what answer. The viewer's `Knows` panel shows "for this lot in your bag: 3 consultations done, 2 axes still uncertain" — the notebook surfaces what's left to learn.
  - **All these axes ultimately resolve into three numbers per item.** Everything above — identification, condition, flaws, price knowledge, customer fit — exists to inform just three actionable prices:
    1. **End-user RRP** — what someone who wants this thing pays. The household-customer price. Today: bidder's noisy `valuation`. The player's confidence band on this number is shaped by their *combined* knowledge across id + condition + flaw + price axes.
    2. **Wholesale value** — what another dealer would pay to take it on. Today implicit: `RRP × pubBuyerCeilingFraction`. But it's not really one number; it varies per potential dealer-buyer (their bidder profile, cash, customer fit). The player's estimate is "what could I move this on for if I sold to Boyce / Monkey-Harris / off-map?" — a band per plausible buyer.
    3. **Acquired cost** — what you paid. Perfectly known from `acquiredUnitPrice`.
  - **The viewer's job becomes showing all three with bands.** Each lot in your bag displays three lines: "Cost £8/unit. Resale (you reckon) £15–£60. End-user £25–£100." Bands shrink as you pay for consultations. Margin is the difference between (1)/(2) and (3); the wider the bands the riskier the bet. Conflicting consultations show diverging bands ("Mickey said £20–£25, Boyce said £180–£200 — somebody's wrong"). This collapses the whole appraisal/knowledge/consultation/notebook stack into a single, comprehensible UI: three numbers per item, each a confidence band, each narrowable by paid actions, each grounded in the underlying knowledge axes the engine already models.
